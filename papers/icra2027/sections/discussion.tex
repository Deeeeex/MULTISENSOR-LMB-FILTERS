\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
ER uses observation history to select participating inputs and direct
sensing age to weight existence, while retaining ordinary spatial weights.
In the robot case studies, this separation reduces set error
relative to ER without recency. In split--rejoin and churn--departure, it
also avoids the common-target localization degradation of applying age to
both blocks. A robot's direct sensing age
and the spatial information accumulated in its posterior describe different
properties: earlier observations and relayed evidence can remain useful
after direct contact ends.

False-track rejection introduces a further tradeoff. ER retains some false
hypotheses, and the two-hit confirmation gate reduces no-new false cost at
the expense of discovery during short sensing contacts. The departure and
acquisition results show why set error, localization, false cost, and
discovery delay should be evaluated together.

The real-detection replay broadens the evidence beyond these prescribed
events and removes shared truth-informed birth schedules. It also exposes
a transfer limit: the complete ER configuration improves on the evaluated
external adaptations, but its age term adds no consistent OSPA gain over
the no-age ablation and increases false cost. The case-study effect should
therefore be interpreted as conditional on sensing and contact history.

The replay uses two vehicles, a cropped planar task, approximate Gaussian
detector likelihoods, moving ego coordinates without motion compensation,
and emulated radio loss. It establishes neither full 3-D benchmark ranking
nor performance in a larger real robot network. Label matching and detector
calibration also affect existence decisions. Improving that calibration and
testing contact-dependent confirmation are concrete next steps before
claiming a broadly transferable recency benefit.
